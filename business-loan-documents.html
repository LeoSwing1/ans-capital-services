What an underwriter actually reads in your business file

Turnover gets the attention. The current account statement decides the outcome.

Business owners usually prepare the balance sheet and overlook the bank statement. Underwriters read them in the opposite order.

The current account tells the real story

Twelve months of banking shows what financial statements summarise: whether money comes in steadily, whether cheques bounce, whether the balance goes negative at month end, whether large cash deposits appear without a matching invoice trail. Each of those affects pricing, and several together affect whether the file is taken at all.

If you are planning to borrow in the next year, the cheapest thing you can do is keep six clean months of banking before you apply.

GST filings are now an income document

Consistent, timely returns that match your declared turnover open programmes that will not look at an irregular filer, whatever the profit figure says. Irregular filing is read as a control problem, not a paperwork problem.

The trade-off nobody mentions

Businesses that minimise declared profit to reduce tax also minimise the income a lender can assess. You cannot show a thin return to the tax authority and a strong one to the underwriter. If borrowing is likely within a year or two, that trade-off is worth thinking about before the returns are filed, not after.

Separate the business and personal accounts

Mixed accounts make cash flow unreadable. An underwriter who cannot read your cash flow prices for the worst case. A clean, separate current account genuinely moves your rate.

Match the instrument to the need

A term loan with a fixed instalment suits an asset you will use for years. For stock or receivables that move with the season, an overdraft — where interest applies only to what you actually draw — is usually far cheaper. Taking a term loan for working capital and parking the surplus in a current account means paying interest on money sitting idle.
